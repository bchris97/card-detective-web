How the calculator decides

Grading has two possible results, and the calculator prices both. If the card comes back in the grade you hoped for, you gain the graded price minus what you paid to grade it and the raw card you gave up. If it comes back a step lower, you get the lower graded price instead, which is often close to the raw value, so the fee is lost.

expected gain = (chance × good-grade price) + ((1 − chance) × lower-grade price) − raw value − grading cost

It says Grade it when the expected gain is positive and worth the trouble (at least $10 and a quarter of the raw value), Toss-up when it's only slightly positive, and Don't grade when you'd expect to lose money.

What grading really costs

The grading fee is only part of it. Count all of these per card:

  • The grading fee, which depends on the service level and, at most companies, on the card's declared value. Higher-value cards cost more to grade.
  • Shipping and insurance both ways. Sending one card at a time makes this a big share of the cost; it's cheaper per card in a batch.
  • Supplies: card savers or semi-rigid holders and a sturdy box.
  • Time. Turnaround can run from weeks to months at the cheapest service levels, and prices can move while you wait.

When grading usually pays off

  • The card is in near-perfect condition and the top grades sell for several times the raw price.
  • Key rookie cards and popular players or characters, where buyers specifically look for graded copies.
  • Older cards in unusually good condition, because few survived that way.
  • You plan to sell, and buyers of that card pay more for a slab they can trust.

When to keep it raw

  • The raw card is worth less than the grading cost. Even a strong grade rarely rescues it.
  • You can see a flaw: a soft corner, whitening on the edges, a scratch, or uneven borders.
  • Graded copies of that card are plentiful and sell for little more than raw ones.
  • You're keeping it for yourself and don't need the label.

Check the condition before you send it

Look at the card under a bright light, ideally with a magnifier, and tilt it to catch the surface.

  • Centering: compare the borders left to right and top to bottom, front and back.
  • Corners: all four should be sharp, with no white showing and no rounding.
  • Edges: no chipping or white specks, especially on dark borders.
  • Surface: no scratches, print lines, dents, creases or stains. Shiny cards hide scratches until you tilt them.

If you find anything, pick "Unlikely" in the calculator and see whether grading still makes sense.

Where to find the numbers

The best inputs are recent sold prices for your exact card: same year, set, card number and parallel. Asking prices, what sellers are listing a card for right now, usually run higher than what cards actually sell for, so using them makes grading look better than it is.

Questions

Is it worth grading a card worth less than $20?

Usually not. Once you add the grading fee and shipping and insurance both ways, a low-value card has to jump a lot in value just to break even, and a mid grade often sells for about the same as the raw card. The exceptions are cards whose top grades sell for many times the raw price.

Does grading always increase a card's value?

No. Grading adds value when the card comes back in a high grade that buyers pay extra for. A lower grade can be worth the same as the raw card, or less once fees are counted, because the flaws are now printed on the label.

Which prices should I use in the calculator?

Use recent sold prices for the same card where you can find them. Asking prices, what sellers list cards for, usually run higher than what cards actually sell for, so they make grading look better than it is. How do I know if my card will get a high grade?

Check four things under good light: centering (are the borders even on all sides?), corners (sharp, no white or rounding), edges (no chipping or whitening) and surface (no scratches, print lines, dents or stains). A single visible flaw usually rules out the top grades.
